Privacy Policy for Mousehole

Effective [date]

Mousehole (“the app”) is a personal security tool made by MouseWurX, a sole proprietorship in Alberta, Canada (“we”). It helps you check what information about you is exposed online and on your phone. This policy explains what happens to your information when you use it.

The short version

  • We don’t run a server, and we don’t collect, see, sell or share any of your information.
  • The app has no ads, no analytics, no tracking and no account.
  • Everything you enter and every result stays on your phone, encrypted.
  • When you run a check, the app contacts the outside service that does that check, and sends it only what that check needs. Those services are listed below.
  • Uninstalling the app deletes everything.

What the app stores on your phone

  • Your profile: the names, email addresses, usernames, phone numbers, domains and cities you choose to enter.
  • Results: findings, breach and leak records, your answers to “Is this you?”, re-check history and your Account Cleanup status.
  • API keys you add for optional services (Have I Been Pwned, IPinfo, Shodan, GitHub).

This information is encrypted with keys held in your phone’s Android Keystore, which never leave the device. Sensitive leaked values are hidden until you unlock them with your fingerprint or screen lock. The app turns off Android cloud backup and phone-to-phone transfer for its data, so none of it is copied to Google’s servers or another phone.

What the app sends, and to whom

A check only runs when you start it, or when you turn on automatic checks or re-checks. Each request goes directly from your phone to the service over an encrypted (HTTPS) connection. The one exception is Home Exposure’s router check, which talks only to your own router inside your home network (see below). Like any website, each service can see your phone’s IP address and the app’s name in the request. Their own privacy policies apply to what they receive.

Some tools don’t send anything themselves but open a website in your browser when you tap a button, such as Reverse Image (Google Lens and other image search engines), Search Kit searches, data broker search and opt-out pages, account deletion pages, and app store links. What you do on those websites is covered by their policies. Reverse Image removes the photo’s location and camera details before you share it.

The Check it yourself buttons work the same way. They open Have I Been Pwned, XposedOrNot, Hudson Rock, Intelligence X, Shodan, IPinfo, crt.sh, Google News, ransomware.live, RansomLook or WiGLE in your browser, on the site’s own page. The app puts what you’re checking (an email, username, domain, IP address, city or router ID) either in the page address or on your clipboard for you to paste. The site then sees that search, and it stays in your browser’s history. The app doesn’t read those pages or keep their results.

What stays on your phone and is never sent

Photo and document metadata reading and cleaning, Photo Location Sweep, Bluetooth tracker detection and Tracker Following, home device discovery on your Wi-Fi, phone number checks, App Permission Review, Clipboard Cleaner, Secure Delete, Google Takeout and Meta export reading, scoring and advice all run entirely on the phone.

Permissions and why the app asks

  • Internet and network state: to run online checks and test your connection.
  • Nearby devices (Bluetooth): to detect tracking tags near you. The app tells Android it never uses Bluetooth scans to work out where you are. Tracker Following judges whether a tag moves with you from the phone’s motion sensor, not from your location, and records no places.
  • Location (precise and approximate): only for Wi-Fi on the Map. Android shows an app your router’s hardware address (BSSID) only if it holds location permission. The app never reads your position; the router’s address is sent to WiGLE only when you tap Check.
  • Photos: the app asks for no photo permission. Photo Location Sweep reads only the folder or the photos you pick in Android’s file picker, and you can make it forget the folder at any time.
  • Wi-Fi state and multicast: to find devices on your home network, ask your router which ports it has opened, and read your router’s ID.
  • Notifications and foreground services: to tell you about new findings and to show progress for long jobs such as tracker watching (which you start yourself and which stops after 8 hours) and free-space wiping.
  • Biometrics: to unlock hidden leaked values.

Every permission is optional. If you say no, only the feature that needs it stops working.

Searching other people

The app is made for checking yourself, and you agree to that when you first open it. Only enter information that belongs to you. Don’t use the app to look up, locate, track or watch anyone else.

Your consent

Before the app runs any check, it shows what it reads and what it sends where, and asks you to agree. You can withdraw that at any time in Settings › About › Privacy; automatic checks then stop and the app asks again before it does anything.

Keeping and deleting your information

Your information stays on your phone until you delete it. You can clear findings and profile entries inside the app, clear the app’s storage in Android Settings, or uninstall the app, which removes everything. Because we never receive your information, we have nothing to delete on our side.

Children

The app is not directed at children under 13, and we don’t knowingly collect information from anyone, including children.

Google Play

If you download the app from Google Play, Google handles the download and any payment under its own privacy policy. If you agree to share crash reports with developers in your phone’s settings, Google may give us anonymous crash and performance statistics through Play Console. These don’t include what you entered in the app.

If you buy Mousehole Pro, Google Play takes the payment. To know whether Pro is unlocked, the app asks Google Play on your phone which purchases your Google account owns and keeps a yes/no answer on the phone. We never see your card or payment details, and we don’t run a server that records purchases.

Your rights

Canadian privacy law (PIPEDA, and Alberta’s Personal Information Protection Act), and laws in other places such as the EU GDPR and California’s CCPA, give you rights to access, correct and delete personal information a business holds about you. Since we hold none, any request will confirm that. You can contact us, or the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada, with any concern.

Security

The app encrypts stored data, uses only encrypted connections to online services, and keeps no copies off your phone. The router check uses UPnP, which routers only offer unencrypted, so the app sends it only to a private address on your home network and never over the internet. No method is perfect. If you find a security problem, please report it to [security email].

Changes to this policy

If the app starts sending information anywhere new, we will update this page and the date at the top before that version is released, and mention it in the app’s release notes.
